Bi-exponential fluorescence decay

When two fluorescent probes are excited by the same wavelength but have different deactivation time constants (τ₁ ≠ τ₂), the measured signal is the linear sum of two single-exponential decays. Fitting: within the displayed time window, a single exponential B·e−t/τ is fit to the sum curve by least squares (in intensity space); both τfit and the amplitude B are optimized. The fitted-lifetime error = (τfit − ⟨τ⟩) / ⟨τ⟩, where ⟨τ⟩ is the amplitude-weighted mean lifetime (A₁τ₁+A₂τ₂)/(A₁+A₂); it measures how far the single-exponential fit's lifetime deviates from the true mean. The curve's relative error is evaluated only where the signal exceeds 1% of the peak, to avoid dividing by near-zero values in the tail. The 1/e reference line is drawn at 1/e of the initial intensity (0.368 when normalized): where a single-exponential curve crosses it, the time coordinate equals that curve's lifetime τ, so you can read it off directly. When normalization is on, the fitted curve is also normalized to 1 at t=0. Rule of thumb: the closer r is to 1, or the more one component dominates in amplitude, the smaller the error and the better a single exponential works. When r is large and both amplitudes are comparable, the error grows (the semi-log curve bends more) and the signal should not be treated as single-exponential.
